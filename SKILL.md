# SEO Competitor Intelligence

## Purpose

This skill analyzes publicly available competitor website evidence and converts the findings into actionable SEO intelligence.

The purpose is not to produce a generic SEO audit.

The purpose is to understand how competitors are using their websites to capture search demand and then identify practical opportunities for the user.

The analysis should answer four questions:

1. What is the competitor doing?
2. What evidence supports the finding?
3. What opportunities or weaknesses can be identified?
4. What should the user do next?

The skill must distinguish clearly between observed evidence, analytical inference, and strategic recommendations.

---

## When to Use

Use this skill when the user wants to:

- Analyze a competitor website for SEO
- Understand a competitor's SEO strategy
- Analyze competitor website architecture
- Identify competitor content structures
- Identify competitor topic clusters
- Identify keyword themes from public content
- Analyze search intent
- Find competitor content gaps
- Identify competitor strengths and weaknesses
- Discover SEO opportunities
- Prioritize SEO opportunities
- Identify internal linking opportunities
- Build a 30/60/90-day SEO roadmap
- Compare multiple competitor websites

Typical inputs may include:

- One or more competitor URLs
- A target website URL
- A business or product category
- A target market
- A target language
- Target topics
- Target keywords
- A specific SEO objective

---

## When Not to Use

Do not use this skill when:

- The user only wants general SEO education
- The task does not involve competitor research
- There is no meaningful competitor website or public evidence to analyze
- The user requests private or confidential competitor information
- The user requests hacked, leaked, stolen, or unauthorized information
- The user requests fabricated SEO metrics
- The requested conclusion cannot reasonably be supported by available evidence

When evidence is unavailable, state the limitation clearly instead of inventing information.

---

## Core Principles

### 1. Evidence First

Important conclusions should be based on publicly observable evidence whenever possible.

Useful evidence may include:

- Page titles
- URLs
- Headings
- Navigation structures
- Breadcrumbs
- Category structures
- Visible page content
- Internal links
- Product or service pages
- Blog posts
- Guides
- Comparison pages
- FAQ sections
- Public metadata
- Public sitemap information
- Public index pages
- Repeated page templates
- Repeated URL patterns

Every major finding should be classified conceptually as one of:

- **Observed** — directly visible or verifiable
- **Inferred** — a reasonable interpretation of observed evidence
- **Recommended** — a strategic action derived from the findings

Do not present an inference as an observed fact.

---

### 2. Public Evidence Only

Use publicly accessible information.

Do not attempt to obtain or infer private information such as:

- Private analytics
- Private Search Console data
- Private customer information
- Private revenue
- Credentials
- Restricted databases
- Hacked information
- Leaked confidential information

The skill should analyze the competitor's public web presence, not attempt to access private systems.

---

### 3. Do Not Fabricate Metrics

Never present unsupported values as factual SEO metrics.

Do not invent:

- Organic traffic
- Search volume
- Keyword rankings
- Conversion rates
- Backlink counts
- Domain authority
- Revenue
- User counts
- Click-through rates
- Conversion data
- Search visibility percentages

If a metric cannot be verified from an available reliable source, explicitly state:

> Not publicly verified.

If an estimate is useful, label it as an estimate and explain the basis of the estimate.

---

### 4. Analyze Patterns, Not Isolated Pages

A competitor's SEO strategy should not be inferred from one page alone when broader evidence is available.

Look for recurring patterns across the website, including:

- URL structures
- Page types
- Topic clusters
- Navigation
- Category structures
- Content hubs
- Internal linking
- Commercial pages
- Informational pages
- Comparison pages
- Programmatic or scalable landing pages
- Repeated content templates

The objective is to understand the competitor's overall content and search strategy.

# Research Workflow

Follow the research workflow in order unless the user's request requires a narrower analysis.

The workflow should move from evidence collection to analysis and then to recommendations.

Do not make strategic recommendations before establishing the relevant competitor evidence.

## Step 1: Define the Research Scope

Before analyzing a competitor, establish the research scope.

Identify the following information when available:

- Competitor URL or URLs
- Target website URL
- Business category
- Main products or services
- Target market
- Target country or region
- Target language
- Target audience
- SEO objective
- Specific topics or keywords

If multiple competitors are provided, analyze each competitor independently before producing cross-competitor conclusions.

If important information is missing but the analysis can still be performed reliably, proceed with clearly stated assumptions.

If missing information would materially change the analysis, ask the user for clarification.

## Step 2: Inspect the Competitor Website

Inspect publicly accessible areas of the competitor website.

Prioritize the following:

1. Homepage
2. Main navigation
3. Primary category pages
4. Product or service pages
5. Blog or resource sections
6. Guides and educational content
7. Comparison pages
8. FAQ pages
9. Important landing pages
10. Public sitemap or index pages when available

Do not assume that the first few pages represent the entire website.

For large websites, inspect representative pages from different sections and page types.

Record the research scope so that the final report clearly explains what was reviewed.

## Step 3: Collect Evidence

Collect observable evidence before making strategic conclusions.

Useful evidence includes:

- Page URLs
- Page titles
- Headings
- Navigation elements
- Breadcrumbs
- Visible content
- Internal links
- Category structures
- Page templates
- URL patterns
- Public metadata
- Public sitemap information
- Repeated content structures

For every important finding, distinguish between:

- Observed evidence
- Analytical inference
- Strategic recommendation

For important findings, preserve the source URL or page reference whenever available.

Evidence records should preferably include:

- Evidence type
- Source URL
- Page title
- Relevant observation
- Analytical interpretation

Do not present an inference as a directly observed fact.

## Step 4: Analyze Website Architecture

Analyze the competitor website as a connected system rather than as a collection of isolated pages.

Identify:

- Primary page types
- Main categories
- Subcategories
- URL structures
- Content hubs
- Product structures
- Service structures
- Blog structures
- Guide structures
- Comparison structures
- FAQ structures
- Location-based pages
- Programmatic or scalable landing pages
- Other repeated page templates

Look for relationships between different page types.

The objective is to understand how the competitor organizes information, content, products, services, and search-oriented pages.

## Step 5: Identify Page Types

Identify the major page types used across the competitor website.

Common page types include:

- Homepage
- Category page
- Product page
- Service page
- Blog post
- Guide
- Tutorial
- Comparison page
- Review page
- FAQ page
- Glossary page
- Location page
- Industry page
- Use-case page
- Landing page

For each important page type, determine:

- Primary purpose
- Target audience
- Main topics
- Likely search intent
- Relationship to other page types
- Whether the pattern appears repeatedly

Do not assume that repeated pages are automatically programmatic.

Use observable evidence such as URL patterns, templates, navigation, and repeated content structures.

## Step 6: Analyze URL Structures

Inspect URL patterns for clues about the site's information architecture.

Look for:

- Directory structures
- Category paths
- Topic paths
- Product paths
- Service paths
- Location paths
- Language paths
- Comparison paths
- Blog paths
- Guide paths
- Repeated URL templates

URL structure can provide evidence about how content is organized.

However, URL structure alone is not proof of SEO performance.

Use URL evidence together with page content, navigation, internal links, and page templates.

## Step 7: Analyze Navigation

Review the competitor's:

- Header navigation
- Footer navigation
- Breadcrumbs
- Category navigation
- Sidebar navigation
- Related-content navigation
- Contextual links

Identify:

- Prominent content sections
- Important commercial pages
- Topic hubs
- Supporting informational content
- Potentially isolated sections
- Repeated navigation patterns

Describe the observable structure.

Do not claim that a navigation element directly improves search rankings unless reliable evidence supports that claim.

## Step 8: Identify Content Hubs

Determine whether the competitor organizes related pages into recognizable topic or content hubs.

A content hub may contain:

- A central topic page
- Supporting articles
- Guides
- Comparison pages
- Product or service pages
- Related internal links

For each significant content hub, identify:

- Main topic
- Supporting topics
- Representative pages
- Likely search intents
- Commercial relationship
- Internal linking relationships

The objective is to understand how the competitor builds and connects topical coverage.

Do not assume that a group of related pages is a formal content hub unless the website structure provides reasonable evidence for that conclusion.

## Step 9: Establish Research Limitations

Before producing strategic conclusions, record important limitations in the available evidence.

Possible limitations include:

- Only publicly accessible pages were reviewed.
- The entire website could not be exhaustively analyzed.
- Only representative pages were sampled.
- Search rankings were not independently verified.
- Organic traffic data was not publicly available.
- Search volume was not independently verified.
- Backlink data was not independently verified.
- Some website sections may not have been accessible.
- Conclusions are based on the pages and evidence that were available during the research.

Do not hide important limitations.

The final analysis should clearly distinguish between what was verified, what was inferred, and what could not be determined.

# Topic and Search Intent Analysis

## Step 10: Identify Topic Themes

Identify the major topics covered by the competitor based on publicly observable website content.

Focus on recurring themes rather than isolated keywords.

Look for topics appearing across:

- Category pages
- Product pages
- Service pages
- Blog posts
- Guides
- Comparison pages
- FAQ pages
- Landing pages
- Other recurring content types

Group related topics into meaningful topic clusters.

For each important topic cluster, identify:

- Topic name
- Representative pages
- Related subtopics
- Target audience
- Likely search intent
- Business relevance
- Content depth
- Strategic importance

Do not claim that a topic is a high-volume keyword unless search-volume evidence is available.

## Step 11: Identify Keyword Themes

Extract keyword themes from publicly observable evidence.

Useful evidence includes:

- Page titles
- Headings
- URL structures
- Navigation labels
- Anchor text
- Repeated terminology
- Product or service names
- Comparison terminology
- FAQ questions
- Topic-specific language

Organize keywords into themes instead of producing an unstructured keyword list.

Possible keyword-theme categories include:

- Core product or service terms
- Problem-based terms
- Feature-related terms
- Comparison terms
- Alternative terms
- Use-case terms
- Audience-specific terms
- Location-specific terms
- Educational terms
- Commercial investigation terms

Do not report estimated search volume, ranking position, traffic, or keyword difficulty unless those values are supported by reliable evidence.

## Step 12: Analyze Topic Coverage

Evaluate how extensively the competitor covers each important topic.

Consider:

- Number and variety of relevant pages
- Content depth
- Supporting subtopics
- Commercial coverage
- Informational coverage
- Comparison coverage
- Internal relationships between pages
- Presence of dedicated topic hubs

Classify topic coverage using qualitative labels such as:

- Strong
- Moderate
- Limited
- Missing

These labels describe the observed content landscape and should not be treated as search-engine rankings.

## Step 13: Analyze Search Intent

Classify the likely search intent associated with important topics and page groups.

Use the following intent categories:

- Informational
- Commercial investigation
- Transactional
- Navigational
- Mixed intent

Use observable evidence to support the classification.

### Informational Intent

Typical evidence may include:

- Definitions
- Explanations
- Tutorials
- Educational guides
- How-to content
- Problem-solving content

### Commercial Investigation Intent

Typical evidence may include:

- Comparisons
- Reviews
- Alternatives
- Product evaluations
- Feature comparisons
- "Best" or "top" style content

### Transactional Intent

Typical evidence may include:

- Pricing
- Purchase pages
- Signup pages
- Service pages
- Product pages
- Conversion-focused landing pages

### Navigational Intent

Typical evidence may include:

- Brand-specific pages
- Product-specific pages
- Company-specific pages
- Login or account pages

### Mixed Intent

Use mixed intent when a topic clearly serves more than one search purpose.

For example, a page may provide educational information while also directing users toward a product or service.

## Step 14: Connect Topics to Search Intent

For important topic clusters, connect the topic to its likely search intent.

Use this structure when useful:

| Topic | Representative Page | Likely Intent | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Topic A | Example page | Informational | Educational content |
| Topic B | Example page | Commercial investigation | Comparison content |
| Topic C | Example page | Transactional | Product or service page |

The classification is an analytical interpretation of publicly observable content.

Do not claim that a search engine officially assigns the intent classification.

## Step 15: Identify Intent Coverage

Evaluate whether the competitor covers the major stages of the search journey.

Consider:

- Awareness and education
- Problem exploration
- Solution research
- Product or service comparison
- Purchase or conversion
- Post-purchase or supporting information

Identify areas where the competitor appears particularly strong or weak.

The goal is to understand whether the competitor has built a connected search-intent ecosystem rather than simply publishing individual pages.

## Step 16: Identify Topic-to-Commercial Relationships

Analyze how informational topics connect to commercial pages.

Look for relationships such as:

- Educational content linking to products
- Guides linking to services
- Comparison pages linking to commercial pages
- Use-case pages linking to relevant products
- FAQ content supporting commercial pages

Only report an existing relationship when it has been observed.

If a relationship is recommended rather than observed, label it as a recommendation.

## Step 17: Record Topic and Intent Limitations

Clearly document limitations that affect topic or intent analysis.

Examples include:

- Keyword rankings were not verified.
- Search volume was not verified.
- Keyword difficulty was not verified.
- Search-engine result pages were not independently analyzed.
- Topic clusters are derived from public website evidence.
- Search intent classifications are analytical judgments.
- A limited sample may not represent the entire competitor website.

Do not convert these analytical limitations into unsupported quantitative claims.

# Competitive Analysis

## Step 18: Analyze Competitive Strengths

Identify strengths that are supported by observable evidence from the competitor website.

Potential strengths include:

- Strong topical coverage
- Clear website architecture
- Well-organized content hubs
- Strong search-intent coverage
- Strong commercial page coverage
- Useful comparison content
- Comprehensive educational content
- Consistent page templates
- Strong internal linking
- Scalable content structures
- Clear user journeys
- Strong connections between informational and commercial content

For each important strength, provide:

- Strength
- Supporting evidence
- Relevant pages or page types
- Strategic implication

Do not describe a website as "strong" simply because it contains many pages.

Page quantity alone is not sufficient evidence of SEO strength.

---

## Step 19: Analyze Competitive Weaknesses

Identify weaknesses based on observable gaps, inconsistencies, or limitations in the competitor's public website.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Missing topics
- Limited topic coverage
- Thin or shallow content
- Weak information architecture
- Weak internal linking
- Poor search-intent coverage
- Missing commercial content
- Weak comparison coverage
- Poorly connected content
- Repetitive page structures
- Poorly differentiated pages
- Missing supporting content
- Weak user journeys

For each important weakness, provide:

- Weakness
- Supporting evidence
- Relevant pages or page types
- Strategic implication

Do not classify something as a weakness merely because the competitor uses a different strategy.

A weakness should have a clear relationship to the user's SEO objective or to an identifiable opportunity.

---

## Step 20: Compare Competitive Strengths and Weaknesses

When useful, compare the competitor's strengths and weaknesses in a structured format.

Example:

| Area | Evidence | Strength or Weakness | Strategic Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Topic Coverage | Multiple related content sections | Strength | Strong topical coverage |
| Comparison Content | Few comparison pages observed | Weakness | Potential content opportunity |
| Internal Linking | Strong connections between related pages | Strength | Useful topic relationships |
| Commercial Coverage | Limited supporting content | Weakness | Opportunity to improve intent coverage |

Use qualitative conclusions.

Do not convert these observations into unsupported numerical SEO scores.

# Content Gap Analysis

## Step 21: Identify Content Gaps

Identify topics or content opportunities where the competitor appears to have:

- No meaningful coverage
- Limited coverage
- Weak coverage
- Outdated coverage
- Poorly structured coverage
- Poor search-intent alignment
- Weak commercial connection
- Weak internal-link support

Content gaps should be based on evidence rather than assumptions.

A useful content gap generally connects:

1. A relevant audience need
2. A meaningful topic or search intent
3. Evidence of weak or missing competitor coverage
4. A realistic opportunity for the user's website

---

## Step 22: Classify Content Gaps

Classify each important gap using one of the following categories:

- Missing
- Weak
- Outdated
- Poorly structured
- Poorly aligned with search intent
- Commercially underdeveloped
- Poorly connected through internal links

Use the category that best describes the evidence.

Do not label a topic "missing" if the competitor actually has relevant content covering it.

---

## Step 23: Evaluate Content Gap Evidence

For each important content gap, identify:

- Gap
- Competitor evidence
- Relevant topic
- Likely search intent
- Audience need
- Business relevance
- Recommended response
- Priority

Use a structure such as:

| Gap | Evidence | Intent | Opportunity | Recommendation | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Topic A | Limited competitor coverage | Informational | High | Create comprehensive guide | High |
| Topic B | Weak comparison coverage | Commercial investigation | Medium | Create comparison content | Medium |

Priority should reflect the evidence and strategic context rather than an invented search-volume number.

---

## Step 24: Distinguish True Gaps from Differences

Do not automatically treat every difference between websites as a content gap.

A competitor may intentionally choose not to cover a topic because:

- It is outside its business model
- It targets a different audience
- The topic has limited commercial relevance
- The topic does not support its product strategy
- The topic is not appropriate for its market

Before identifying a gap, consider whether the topic is relevant to the user's own business and audience.

---

## Step 25: Identify Cross-Competitor Content Gaps

When analyzing multiple competitors, identify:

- Topics missing across most competitors
- Topics weakly covered across most competitors
- Topics covered strongly by only one competitor
- Topics where competitors use different approaches
- Topics that appear strategically important but receive limited coverage

Prioritize gaps that remain underserved across the competitive landscape.

Do not assume that a topic is valuable solely because competitors do not cover it.

The topic must also have relevance to the user's audience and business objectives.

---

## Step 26: Content Gap Limitations

Clearly state limitations affecting content-gap analysis.

Examples include:

- Competitor websites may contain content that was not discovered during the research.
- Search demand was not independently verified.
- Search volume was not independently verified.
- Competitor rankings were not independently verified.
- Business value depends on the user's specific business model.
- A content gap does not automatically mean that creating a page will produce rankings or traffic.

Content gaps should therefore be treated as strategic opportunities for further validation, not guaranteed SEO wins.

# SEO Opportunity Analysis

## Step 27: Identify SEO Opportunities

Convert the findings from the competitor analysis into actionable SEO opportunities.

An SEO opportunity should connect:

1. An observed competitor pattern or gap
2. A relevant topic, audience need, or search intent
3. A potential strategic benefit
4. A realistic action the user can take

Potential opportunities include:

- Creating missing content
- Improving weak content
- Expanding topic coverage
- Building topic hubs
- Creating comparison content
- Creating commercial-intent content
- Improving internal linking
- Improving content structure
- Creating supporting pages
- Strengthening connections between informational and commercial content
- Developing scalable content structures

Do not describe an opportunity as guaranteed to produce rankings, traffic, or revenue.

---

## Step 28: Prioritize SEO Opportunities

Prioritize SEO opportunities using two separate dimensions:

### Strategic Priority

Strategic Priority measures how important an opportunity is to the overall SEO and business strategy.

Evaluate Strategic Priority using the following four dimensions:

- Business Value
- Search Intent Value
- Competitive Gap
- Strategic Value

Use a 1–5 scale for each dimension:

- 1 = Low
- 2 = Limited
- 3 = Moderate
- 4 = High
- 5 = Very High

When a numerical Strategic Priority score is useful, calculate it as:

Strategic Priority Score = Business Value + Search Intent Value + Competitive Gap + Strategic Value

The maximum Strategic Priority Score is 20.

The score is an internal strategic prioritization framework.

It does not represent:

- Google ranking probability
- Search volume
- Traffic potential
- Revenue potential
- Conversion probability

### Implementation Effort

Evaluate Implementation Effort separately from Strategic Priority.

Use a 1–5 scale:

- 1 = Very easy
- 2 = Easy
- 3 = Moderate
- 4 = Difficult
- 5 = Very difficult

A higher Implementation Effort score means that the opportunity requires more resources, time, technical work, content production, or organizational coordination.

Implementation Effort must not be subtracted directly from Strategic Priority.

A high-value opportunity with high execution effort should not automatically be considered low priority.

Instead, Strategic Priority and Implementation Effort should be used together to determine implementation sequencing.

For example:

- High Strategic Priority + Low Effort → Execute early
- High Strategic Priority + Moderate Effort → Execute early or in the first major implementation phase
- High Strategic Priority + High Effort → Plan as a strategic project and begin when dependencies are ready
- Moderate Strategic Priority + Low Effort → Consider quick wins
- Moderate Strategic Priority + High Effort → Defer unless strategic conditions change
- Low Strategic Priority + Low Effort → Execute only when resources are available
- Low Strategic Priority + High Effort → Usually defer

Do not use Implementation Effort to artificially reduce the Strategic Priority score.

The purpose of Implementation Effort is to determine sequencing, resource requirements, and execution planning.

If the available evidence is insufficient to score an opportunity reliably, use qualitative labels such as:

- High
- Medium
- Low

and explain the reasoning.

---

## Step 29: Determine Implementation Sequencing

Use Strategic Priority and Implementation Effort together to determine which opportunities should be executed first.

The recommended sequencing logic is:

### Tier 1 - Immediate Opportunities

Characteristics:

- High Strategic Priority
- Low or Moderate Implementation Effort
- Limited dependencies
- Clear business relevance

These opportunities should generally be executed first.

### Tier 2 - Strategic Projects

Characteristics:

- High Strategic Priority
- High Implementation Effort
- Significant business or SEO value
- Clear dependencies or resource requirements

These opportunities should not be downgraded simply because they are difficult.

Instead, they should be planned as strategic projects with defined milestones, dependencies, and resource requirements.

### Tier 3 - Quick Wins

Characteristics:

- Moderate Strategic Priority
- Low Implementation Effort
- Can be implemented without major dependencies

These opportunities may be executed alongside Tier 1 work when they provide efficient incremental value.

### Tier 4 - Planned Opportunities

Characteristics:

- Moderate Strategic Priority
- Moderate or High Implementation Effort
- Useful but not immediately critical

These opportunities should be scheduled after higher-priority initiatives.

### Tier 5 - Deferred Opportunities

Characteristics:

- Low Strategic Priority
- High Implementation Effort
- Weak business relevance
- Significant dependencies without sufficient strategic justification

These opportunities should generally be deferred.

This sequencing framework is a planning tool.

It does not predict rankings, traffic, revenue, or search-engine performance.

When dependencies exist, dependencies may override the default sequencing order.

For example, a high-priority content cluster may need its topic hub and information architecture established before supporting pages can be produced.

---

## Step 30: Build the Opportunity Matrix

Present important opportunities in a structured matrix.

Use the following fields when appropriate:

| Opportunity | Evidence | Business Value | Intent Value | Competitive Gap | Strategic Value | Strategic Priority | Implementation Effort | Execution Tier | Recommended Timing |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|---|
| Opportunity A | Evidence summary | 5 | 5 | 4 | 5 | 19/20 | 2/5 | Tier 1 | 0–30 days |
| Opportunity B | Evidence summary | 5 | 4 | 5 | 5 | 19/20 | 5/5 | Tier 2 | 31–90 days |
| Opportunity C | Evidence summary | 3 | 4 | 4 | 3 | 14/20 | 1/5 | Tier 3 | 0–30 days |

The table above is illustrative only.

The scores are placeholders and must not be reused as actual analysis results.

### Scoring Rules

Strategic Priority Score is calculated as:

Strategic Priority Score = Business Value + Search Intent Value + Competitive Gap + Strategic Value

The maximum score is 20.

Implementation Effort is evaluated separately on a 1–5 scale.

Do not subtract Implementation Effort from Strategic Priority.

The Execution Tier and Recommended Timing should be determined by considering:

- Strategic Priority
- Implementation Effort
- Dependencies
- Available resources
- Business relevance
- Existing website readiness

Do not assign scores merely to make the table look complete.

If the evidence is insufficient for numerical scoring, use qualitative values instead.

# Content Recommendations

## Step 31: Generate Content Recommendations

Convert high-priority opportunities into specific content recommendations.

For each recommendation identify:

- Recommended topic
- Primary search intent
- Target audience
- Recommended page type
- Recommended content angle
- Main sections
- Supporting topics
- Internal-link opportunities
- Commercial relationship
- Priority

Prefer recommendations that can become part of a broader topic cluster.

Avoid producing large lists of isolated article ideas without strategic relationships.

---

## Step 32: Recommend Page Types

Choose the page type that best matches the intended search behavior.

Possible page types include:

- Guide
- Tutorial
- Comparison
- Review
- Alternative page
- Product page
- Service page
- Category page
- Use-case page
- Industry page
- Location page
- FAQ
- Glossary
- Topic hub
- Supporting article

The recommended page type should be based on the likely search intent and business objective.

Do not recommend a transactional page for an obviously informational topic without explaining the strategic reason.

---

## Step 33: Define Content Angles

For important content recommendations, identify how the user's content could provide value beyond simply copying competitor coverage.

Potential differentiation angles include:

- Better organization
- More complete coverage
- Clearer explanations
- Better comparisons
- More practical examples
- More current information
- Better audience targeting
- Stronger commercial usefulness
- Better internal navigation
- More useful supporting resources

Do not recommend copying competitor content.

Use competitor research to identify opportunities, not to reproduce another website's content.

---

## Step 34: Build Topic Clusters

When several recommendations address the same broader topic, organize them into a topic cluster.

A topic cluster may contain:

- One primary hub page
- Several supporting informational pages
- Comparison content
- Commercial pages
- FAQ content
- Supporting use-case pages

For each cluster identify:

- Hub topic
- Supporting topics
- Primary search intents
- Commercial relationship
- Recommended internal links

Prioritize coherent clusters when they provide greater strategic value than isolated pages.

---

# Internal Linking Opportunities

## Step 35: Identify Internal Linking Opportunities

Analyze how the competitor connects related pages and identify opportunities for the user's website.

Potential relationships include:

- Topic hub → supporting page
- Supporting page → topic hub
- Informational page → commercial page
- Comparison page → product or service page
- Product or service page → educational resource
- Use-case page → relevant product or service
- Related topic → related topic

Only state that a link currently exists when it has been observed.

---

## Step 36: Recommend Internal Link Relationships

For each recommended internal-link opportunity, identify:

- Source page or topic
- Destination page or topic
- Link purpose
- Relevant context
- Suggested anchor concept

The recommendation should be contextually useful to the reader.

Do not recommend links solely for manipulating search rankings.

Internal links should help users discover related information and understand the relationship between pages.

---

## Step 37: Distinguish Observed and Recommended Links

Clearly distinguish between:

- Existing link — directly observed on the competitor or user's website
- Potential link — inferred from the content relationship
- Recommended link — a proposed improvement

Never describe a proposed link as an existing link.

---

## Step 38: Internal Linking Limitations

Internal-link recommendations may be limited by:

- Incomplete website discovery
- Limited page sampling
- Dynamic navigation
- JavaScript-rendered content
- Pages that were not publicly accessible
- Content that may exist outside the inspected sections

State these limitations when they materially affect the analysis.

# SEO Roadmap

## Step 39: Build a 30/60/90-Day Roadmap

Convert the highest-priority SEO opportunities into a practical implementation roadmap.

The roadmap should prioritize actions based on:

- Strategic value
- Business relevance
- Search intent
- Competitive gap
- Execution effort
- Dependencies between tasks

Do not present the roadmap as a guarantee of rankings, traffic, or revenue.

---

## Step 40: Define the 0-30 Day Plan

The first phase should focus on high-value actions that can be implemented relatively quickly.

Potential actions include:

- Fixing clearly identified content weaknesses
- Improving existing high-value pages
- Creating important missing pages
- Improving internal linking
- Establishing topic hubs
- Improving page structure
- Addressing clearly observable information-architecture issues

For each action identify:

- Action
- Reason
- Expected strategic benefit
- Relative effort
- Priority

---

## Step 41: Define the 31-60 Day Plan

The second phase should focus on building and expanding strategic coverage.

Potential actions include:

- Creating new topic clusters
- Expanding commercial content
- Creating comparison content
- Creating supporting informational content
- Strengthening internal-link relationships
- Expanding high-value content areas
- Improving connections between informational and commercial pages

Prioritize actions that build on work completed during the first phase.

---

## Step 42: Define the 61-90 Day Plan

The third phase should focus on scaling and refinement.

Potential actions include:

- Expanding successful content patterns
- Building additional topic clusters
- Improving commercial-intent coverage
- Expanding supporting content
- Refining internal-link architecture
- Reviewing available performance data
- Reprioritizing future content based on validated results

Do not claim that a content pattern is successful unless performance evidence is available.

---

## Step 43: Identify Dependencies

When roadmap actions depend on earlier work, make the dependency explicit.

Examples:

- A topic hub may need to exist before supporting articles are created.
- Internal linking may depend on new pages being published.
- Content expansion may depend on identifying the highest-value topic clusters.
- Performance-based prioritization may depend on collecting actual performance data.

The roadmap should reflect these relationships when relevant.

# Multi-Competitor Analysis

## Step 44: Analyze Multiple Competitors

When multiple competitors are provided, analyze each competitor independently before creating a combined analysis.

For each competitor identify:

- Website architecture
- Main topic clusters
- Search-intent coverage
- Competitive strengths
- Competitive weaknesses
- Content gaps
- Internal-linking patterns
- Strategic opportunities

Do not merge evidence from different competitors without identifying the source.

---

## Step 45: Identify Common Competitive Patterns

Compare competitors to identify patterns shared across the market.

Look for:

- Common topic clusters
- Common page types
- Common commercial content
- Common comparison content
- Common information architecture
- Common internal-linking patterns
- Common content gaps

A pattern appearing across multiple competitors may indicate an established market strategy.

However, frequency alone does not prove that the strategy is effective.

---

## Step 46: Identify Competitor-Specific Advantages

Identify areas where one competitor appears meaningfully different from others.

Examples include:

- Deeper coverage of a topic
- Stronger comparison content
- More complete commercial coverage
- More sophisticated content architecture
- More extensive supporting content
- Different search-intent coverage

Explain the evidence supporting the difference.

---

## Step 47: Identify Cross-Competitor Opportunities

Identify opportunities that appear underserved across multiple competitors.

Potential opportunities include:

- Topics missing across competitors
- Weakly covered topics
- Poorly structured content areas
- Weak commercial-intent coverage
- Weak comparison coverage
- Missing supporting content
- Weak connections between content and commercial pages

Do not assume that an underserved topic is automatically valuable.

Validate the topic against:

- User needs
- Business relevance
- Search intent
- Strategic objectives

---

## Step 48: Build a Competitor Comparison Matrix

When useful, summarize the competitive landscape using a comparison matrix.

Example:

| Area | Competitor A | Competitor B | Competitor C | Opportunity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Topic Coverage | Strong | Moderate | Limited | High |
| Commercial Content | Strong | Strong | Moderate | Medium |
| Comparison Content | Moderate | Strong | Limited | High |
| Internal Linking | Strong | Moderate | Limited | High |

Use qualitative descriptions unless reliable quantitative evidence is available.

# Data and Evidence Standards

## Step 50: Separate Facts from Inference

The final report should distinguish among:

**Observed evidence**

Information directly visible or verifiable from the research.

**Inference**

A reasonable analytical conclusion based on observed evidence.

**Recommendation**

An action proposed based on the evidence and analysis.

Use language that makes these distinctions clear.

Examples:

- "The website contains..."
- "This suggests..."
- "A potential opportunity is..."
- "Based on the observed structure..."
- "This was not independently verified..."

Avoid presenting speculative conclusions as facts.

---

## Step 51: Handle Missing Evidence

When information cannot be verified:

- State that it is unavailable.
- Do not substitute an invented value.
- Explain why the limitation matters when relevant.
- Continue the analysis using available evidence where possible.

Examples:

- "Organic traffic was not publicly verified."
- "Keyword rankings were not independently verified."
- "Search volume was not independently verified."
- "The analysis is based on publicly accessible pages."

---

# Quality Control

## Step 52: Validate the Analysis

Before returning the final report, verify that:

- Major findings have supporting evidence.
- Observed evidence is separated from inference.
- Recommendations are connected to findings.
- Content gaps are evidence-based.
- Search-intent classifications are explained.
- Opportunity scores follow the defined framework.
- Roadmap priorities are logically ordered.
- Multi-competitor findings identify their sources.
- Unsupported SEO metrics are not presented as facts.
- Research limitations are disclosed.

---

## Step 53: Check for Unsupported Claims

Before completing the report, specifically check for unsupported claims about:

- Search rankings
- Search volume
- Organic traffic
- Backlinks
- Domain authority
- Revenue
- Conversion rates
- User numbers
- SEO performance

Remove or qualify any claim that cannot be supported.

---

## Step 54: Avoid False Precision

Do not create false precision by assigning exact numbers to uncertain information.

Prefer:

- High
- Medium
- Low
- Strong
- Moderate
- Limited
- Not publicly verified

over unsupported exact values.

When numerical scoring is used, explain that the score is a prioritization framework.

---

## Step 55: Preserve Research Scope

Do not imply that the entire competitor website was analyzed if only a sample was reviewed.

The final report must accurately describe:

- Pages inspected
- Sections inspected
- Sampling limitations
- Evidence availability

---

## Final Response Rules

The final response should be:

- Evidence-based
- Action-oriented
- Transparent about uncertainty
- Structured for practical SEO decision-making
- Specific enough to support implementation
- Conservative about unsupported claims

The goal is to help the user decide what to do next, not simply to describe the competitor.

When evidence is insufficient, say so clearly.

### Ranking positions

Ranking positions must only be reported when supported by reliable evidence. Do not invent, estimate, or imply exact ranking positions when they have not been independently verified.

### Evidence Integrity

Evidence Integrity requires that findings, metrics, ranking positions, search volume, traffic, and other SEO claims are clearly distinguished between verified evidence, analytical inference, and information that could not be independently verified. Unsupported values must not be presented as facts.
